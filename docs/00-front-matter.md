### Foreword

I never camped outdoors with my family, nor did I ever camp when I was a cub scout. The I only things of note from that time in my life are that my den mother was a former Miss Michigan and my father and I enjoyed designing and crafting pinewood derby cars. When I did start camping a few years later, I quickly took to it with great zeal. I enjoyed getting out in the woods with just my gear in my backpack to rely on. Evenings in a tent meant playing games, including a fair number of RPG sessions. RPGs involve storytelling, adventure, mystery, and suspense—all core features of my time in scouting, which continues to this day. When I applied to be a counselor for the Game Design merit badge, I simply listed “Chaosium” in the qualifications section!

Nowadays, I continue to enjoy camping, especially when there’s an opportunity to build a campfire each evening. Building a fire provides a timeless, comforting, and social experience regardless of the weather. Many of my fondest memories as a scout feature a campfire under the stars, the songs we sang, the ghost stories we told, and the skits we laughed at. My favorite scouting skills involve teaching how to gather tinder, kindling, and wood to construct and ultimately light a campfire. When I became my troop’s scoutmaster a little over a decade ago, I swore an oath that I would only light a campfire using flint and steel, and so far, I have honored my promise, even when deterred by rain and snow! If nothing else, it’s a core survival skill that I hope I never _really_ need to use.

As a scout, I learned how to cook, canoe, hike, navigate with a map and compass, forage for food, sail a boat, tie knots, make shelters, identify poisonous plants and dangerous animals, and many other skills that could certainly come in handy as a _Call of Cthulhu_ investigator. You find a different and deeper appreciation for the world when you are out in the wilderness. The night sky is darker and full of more stars, while the sounds are more primitive and sometimes startling. You develop a stronger connection to nature as you ponder its mysteries, its power, and its eternal indifference to our wants and needs. It’s somewhat spooky to hear things like thunder in the distance or the snap of a twig down the trail, or just what creature is staring at you with those glinting eyes from out of the darkness.

I wish I could have experienced the early days of scouting back in the 1920s and 1930s. As a collector of scouting memorabilia, I ponder what stories the items I obtain might tell if they could speak. Being rather nostalgic, the simpler technology, the slower pace of life, and a more rural society all these items harken back to appeals to me. Until time travel becomes possible, I’ll happily settle for playing the scenarios in this book, and enjoying the stories that unfold from them at the gaming table— or possibly at a picnic table on a campout with my troop. And yes, I always have a set of dice in my pack!

—Yours in Scouting, Rick Meints Scoutmaster, Troop 4, and President of Chaosium.

### Preface: the Story of This Project

Normally, I would not spend ink describing the genesis of a project; however, it is a cute story and hopefully, worth a read. While my sons were in the Boy Scouts of America[®] (as I had been), I enjoyed volunteering and being a part of their journey. It allowed me to get back to nature and activities I enjoy, such as hiking and camping. A highlight for the boys was a week at scout camp in northern Wisconsin. On one of those early camp outings, I volunteered to go with the troop and be an in-camp leader for the week.

Now, I had known that several of the boys (including my own) enjoyed playing tabletop roleplaying games and collectible card games. I thought _Call of Cthulhu_ might be fun; it’s easy to learn, and it would allow them to participate in their own ghost story while in the woods. I wrote a fun but deadly oneshot scenario (an early version of **Tremors Below** , page 70) and rolled up investigators. I decided to make the story outdoor and scout-themed, and the investigators, scouts (many feature later in this book).

The last night, we gathered in the dining hall at a large table. I ran them through the scenario, enthralling everyone for hours. We finished sometime after 11 pm, and the walk through the woods to our campsite was quiet. The next day, the children told me how much they had been freaked out on the way home! I thought, _“Oh crap, I am going to get some angry calls from parents.”_ Instead, they were hooked, and I continued to write new adventures for camp and cabin outings over the years. Those scenarios became the basis for this book.

### Introduction

What is it about listening to a spine-tingling ghost story told around a fire in the deep, dark woods that amplifies the scary sensations? What makes the hairs on the back of your neck stand up as a sense of dread builds? Is it simply the cadence of the storyteller and the suspense of the tale? Or is it something much deeper and darker?

Our ancestors huddled around fires for protection, not just to stave off the cold, but also to keep predators away. If you have ever lain awake in a tent, imagining every sound to be some monster about to strike, you have felt that same primal fear.

This book provides an opportunity to experience _Call of Cthulhu_ in a new way: one that helps keep the game fresh and challenges both the Keeper and players to adapt and find new opportunities to roleplay—the true heart of cooperative storytelling games. While the Cthulhu Mythos is woven into the fabric of these stories, it is not the driving force. Many of these scenarios operate more like ghost stories or the setup to a high-action horror movie.

This book allows you to play a junior investigator, full of wild-eyed naïveté and innocence, rather than the usual grizzled veteran. The investigator creation guidelines and backgrounds provided help craft a character who just stepped out of a Norman Rockwell* painting. For anyone who camped growing up, this taps into those primal fears found only in the wild.

* _Normal Rockwell (1894–1978) became famous for his folksy paintings of US life that appeared on the covers of the_ Saturday Evening Post, _but he also made many scoutthemed ones._

### What’s in This Book?

This book contains four linked scenarios that form the Westhaven Campaign. The scout-investigators start the first scenario aged 11, and reach adulthood in the final chapter. At the end, the scout-investigators advance to adult investigators, ready to embark on adventures and campaigns beyond Westhaven— such as the epic _Call of Cthulhu_ campaigns, _Masks of Nyarlathotep_ or _A Time to Harvest_ .

The scout squad is a new investigator organization for _Campfire Tales_ , and the bonds they make within their squad will never be forgotten.

### Content Warning

_Call of Cthulhu_ is a game about horror and mystery, and scenarios may feature such things as evil cults, deadly creatures, graphic violence, gore, death, rotting corpses, and untrustworthy authority figures, among other horrors.

Also, sadly, countless cases of abuse have taken place within various scouting organizations around the world. It would be remiss of us not to acknowledge and condemn such acts. Such things do not feature in this book.

While some groups may want to explore a more visceral, gritty game world, others may desire a subtler approach and avoid certain matters. We recommend Keepers adjust their presentation of this subject matter in accordance with their own and their group’s comfort level. Some excellent free “RPG safety toolkits” are available online—we recommend checking them out to see if they are a good fit for you and your players.

_Campfire Tales_ presents Keepers and players with new tools such as:

- Rules for creating junior investigators—a host of new “hobbies” (occupations) and backstory elements.
- Assisted fighting maneuvers—allowing scoutinvestigators to combine forces in combat.
- Adversity—testing the scout-investigators’ resolve in the face of hunger and fatigue.
- Scout ability badges—each provides a unique in-game bonus.

### Inspiration

Beyond the obvious inspiration found in the real Scout Movement, additional fuel for creativity can be found in other areas. Literature, television, and movies brim with plucky, ragtag groups of youths who go on adventures, face danger, and triumph. One early example from cinema was the _Our Gang_ crew of poor children; these features were among the first to show white and Black children interacting as equals.

Starting in the late 1920s, _The Hardy Boys_ and _Nancy Drew_ books presented wholesome American youth that investigated mysteries—sometimes of a seemingly supernatural cause. While the antagonists were almost always human, the air of mystery and suspense added tension. Between the two, over 300 books appeared in the series.

A big hit on Saturday morning television, _Scooby Doo, Where Are You?_ began in 1969, created by HannaBarbera Productions. This cartoon series featured teenagers Fred Jones, Daphne Blake, Velma Dinkley, Norville “Shaggy” Rogers, and their talking Great Dane named Scooby Doo.

The group solved mysteries involving supposedly supernatural creatures through a series of antics and missteps, while driving around the country in the Mystery Machine (their iconic customized van). While the adventures always teased supernatural creatures, the culprit was always some ne’er-do-well in a rubber mask, lamenting that they _“would’ve gotten away with it if it wasn’t for you meddling kids.”_ Movies and television continued to use these themes, often presented as a story that was a throwback to an earlier era. The journey of a young boy who befriends a stranded alien is chronicled in _E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial_ (1982). _The Goonies_ (1985) follows a group of kids who attempt to save their homes from foreclosure; in the process, they discover a treasure map that leads them to the lost fortune of One-Eyed Willy, a 17[th] -century pirate. _Stand by Me_ (1986), based on Stephen King’s novella _The Body_ , followed four friends’ journey to see a corpse and their resulting coming-of-age adventures.

_Super 8_ (2011) told the story of teenagers filming their own movie when a train derails, releasing a dangerous presence into their town. The story is set in 1979 with heavy period influences. The story plays out like a much scarier version of _E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial,_ but also focuses on themes of loss and regret. More recently, _Stranger Things_ (2016–present) has become a world-wide sensation. Set in the fictional town of Hawkins, Indiana, during the 1980s, the protagonists are a group of kids (who happen to like roleplaying games!). Season One focuses on the investigation into a young boy’s disappearance amid supernatural events, including the appearance of a girl with strange powers.

### History of Scouting

This book’s scenarios present a fictional scouting organization to which the scout-investigators belong. The history of the real-world Scout Movement goes back over 100 years. In that time, it has changed, evolved, and spread across the globe. While the Scout Movement is old, many of its influences are even older—by 150 years or more. It continues in various forms to this day.


### Historical Note

Many of the cinema and stories referenced here often portrayed racial and ethnic stereotypes that are considered problematic by today’s standards—a theme common across much early media and daily life. ### Early Youth Movements

The Young Men’s Christian Association (YMCA) was founded in 1844 by George Williams (1821–1905) and his colleagues. The Industrial Revolution brought many young men to the growing cities. Williams and his cohorts were concerned about the lack of healthy activities for young men in major cities—the most prominent options of the day were usually brothels and taverns. Williams’ idea grew out of prayer meetings he held among his fellow workers in the City of London, England. This led to the founding of the YMCA with the stated purpose of _“the improving of the spiritual condition of young men engaged in the drapery, embroidery, and other trades.”_ William Henry Harrison Murray (1840–1904) authored a series of articles and books about the Adirondack Mountains in upstate New York. He was an ordained minister, delivering sermons in Connecticut and Massachusetts that focused on the outdoors and the Adirondacks. Murray wrote of the north woods of New York as being _“health-giving and spirit-enhancing.”_ He admired the Adirondack woodsmen and became known as “Adirondack Murray”—the father of the Outdoor Movement.

One of the first modern dedicated youth programs was the Woodcraft Indians, founded by Ernest Thompson Seton in 1901. Under Seton’s leadership, the group co-opted Native American myths and iconography (or at least, stylized misconceptions common in that era) to teach its message. Originally for boys, it later expanded to include girls.

Seton instructed the children in outdoor skills (or woodcraft, as it was known then). The program spread
quickly across the globe; and it strongly influenced later youth programs including the Sons of Daniel Boone (founded by Daniel Carter Beard in 1905), the Scout Movement, and the Lone Scouts (founded in 1913 by John Hargrave).

### Scouting’s Founding in England

The founder of the Scout Movement was an Englishman named Robert Baden-Powell. His formative years were spent at Charterhouse, one of England’s most famous public schools (an elite, feepaying institution). While there, his interest in the outdoors took hold and grew. Later, Baden-Powell served as a military officer in the British Army. He was stationed in India (then a part of the British Empire) during the 1880s, and it was in this period that he took a keen interest in military scouting. The lessons he learned culminated in a publication titled _Reconnaissance and Scouting_ (1884).

During the Second Matabele War in Southern Rhodesia (present-day Zimbabwe), Baden-Powell served as chief of staff to Gen. Frederick Carrington. It was during this time that Baden-Powell began a lifelong friendship with Frederick Russell Burnham. Burnham was the chief of scouts for the British Army in Africa at the time. Burnham had been born and raised on a reservation in America to white and Dakota parents. Baden-Powell worked with Burnham on many reconnaissance missions into enemy territory. Many of Baden-Powell’s later ideas concerning the Boy Scouts originated during this period.

Burnham introduced Baden-Powell to woodcraft, which was largely unknown outside of its main practitioners in the US West, including frontiersmen and the indigenous peoples. Often, they discussed the changing nature of warfare in Africa and the need to develop training programs for young soldiers. The tenets of this program would be rooted in woodcraft principles: exploration, tracking, fieldcraft, and self-reliance.

A few years later, Baden-Powell was stationed in the town of Mafeking in present-day South Africa during the Second Boer War. The British troops in Mafeking were under siege by a much larger Boer force for over a year. During that time, the Mafeking Cadet Corps was formed. This freed adult soldiers to concentrate on military duties and provided a positive outlet for the town’s boys. The performance and success of the Cadet Corps inspired Baden-Powell’s later Scout Movement.

### Export to United States (the Bsa[®] )

The progressive movement of the early 20[th] century saw more families shift from rural farming areas to the cities, primarily for greater economic opportunities. This caused some concern that US boys were potentially losing important aspects of their lives: most notably, self-reliance, patriotism, and America’s famed individualism. Several organizations sought to fill that gap including the YMCA, Woodcraft Indians, and the Sons of Daniel Boone.

The saga behind the founding of the Boy Scouts of America[®] (BSA[®] ) has deeply imprinted itself upon the organization’s values.

According to the lore, in 1909, Chicago-based publisher William D. Boyce was visiting London on business when he became lost in a pea-soup fog. While seeking directions, Boyce encountered a boy who helped him navigate the city’s foggy streets. When Boyce attempted to pay the boy, the boy (later referred to as the Unknown Scout), politely declined; he replied that he was a Boy Scout and simply doing a good deed.

Boyce was so impressed he met with staff at the Boy Scouts Association headquarters to learn more. When he returned to the US, he founded the Boy Scouts of America[®] .

The Girl Scouts of the United States of America was founded by Juliette Gordon Low in 1912. Low met Robert Baden-Powell on a trip to England in 1911; she was so impressed with the education provided by the Scout Movement that she brought her version to the US. She started in her hometown, Savannah, Georgia, and it became instantly successful.

### Scouting in Other Countries

Encouraging youth to achieve their full potential, become model citizens in a non-political, educational program without exclusion of race, gender, or socioeconomic background became a powerful 20[th] -century rallying cry. Soon after the founding of the scouting associations in England and the US, similar organizations sprang up around the globe. While many original themes persist, each has taken their own distinct paths, especially regarding co-education.

Although some countries continue separate organizations for boys and girls, other countries, especially in Europe, have merged into one mixedgender organization.

### Goals of Scouting

The worldwide programs collectively referred to as the Scout Movement have unique distinctions based on locale. But they also have similarities. Skills development, respect and care for the natural world, citizenship, and leadership factor into all of them. While some view the scout uniform as “militaristic,” it also ensures that all scouts dress equally, regardless of social or economic standing.

The BSA[®] ’s primary objectives include character development, citizenship, personal fitness, and leadership. These goals are part of an age-appropriate educational system known as the Scout Method. These ideals appear in the Scout Oath, Scout Law, Outdoor Code, Scout Motto (“Be Prepared”[®] ), and slogan (“Do a good turn daily”). Boy Scouts™ share responsibilities, such as cooking and cleaning while camping; and they learn skills like the patrol method, which organizes members into small groups with their own youth leadership. Adults observe, provide role models, and mentor; youth leaders provide skills education and hands-on leadership by example.

Girl Scouts focus on empowerment and leadership, and promoting ideals such as citizenship, confidence, courage, compassion, and entrepreneurship. Also, they emphasize community service and practical skills, such as camping and first aid. Their most famous entrepreneurship activity is the eponymously named “Girl Scout Cookies[®] ” peddled every year by tenacious US girl scouts.

### The Wayfarer Scouts

This book uses a _fictional_ scouting organization named the Wayfarer Scouts, inspired by real scouting groups worldwide.

The Wayfarer Scouts were founded in 1918 by Reginald Corker; Corker grew up in a small farming community outside of Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. Corker’s frequent hunting, fishing, and hiking trips with his father impacted the lad profoundly. They imprinted a lifelong love for nature on the boy.

Captain Corker, as his peers called him, was an experienced US soldier and Great War veteran. He served on the Western Front and at the First Battle of Cambrai, where he fought bravely and received medals and honors. He subsequently invalided out of the army and returned home in 1918 to settle in Arlington, Virginia with his wife, Gertrude. Corker continued to consult with the US Army on training techniques while also taking teaching positions at area colleges.

During the war, Corker came to understand the plight of the young men who fought and died. Some were barely old enough to shave. Many, clearly overwhelmed by the war’s ferocity and horror, could not adapt. So many of these unprepared souls ended up face down in the mud of Flanders Fields, never to return home. Corker realized, if he could find a way to better prepare these young men, fewer would die on the battlefield.

So, Corker ran his idea past business, military, and academic leaders. He sought the brightest minds in developing a program that would deliver his vision. Those discussions led to the formation of the Wayfarer Scouts; a non-profit organization dedicated to preparing the leaders of tomorrow.

His military experience and Pennsylvania adventures convinced him the teaching platform for his organization should be the natural world, wild places especially. As a result, Corker focused the Wayfarer Scouts on outdoor leadership. He believes almost all important lessons that young people need could be learned by getting out into nature. Helping people measure themselves against the harsh, unpredictable wilderness, while also acquiring useful skills, was a challenge he embraced.

At the same time, Corker was the father of three young girls. Progressive socially, he wanted his
daughters to grow into strong, independent women. His wife, Gertrude, supported the suffrage movement, campaigning for women’s right to vote, and her views played a role too. As a result, Corker made the Wayfarer Scouts mixed gender from day one. His time on the battlefield also taught him important lessons about race—a soldier’s courage did not come from the color of their skin. Consequently, he opened the Wayfarer Scouts to all youth, regardless of their background or ethnicity.

The message and experiences of Captain Corker’s new organization resonated with the US youth. By 1919, thousands of boys and girls were participating in the program. This early success led to the development of scout camps where Wayfarer Scouts could spend weeks improving wilderness and leadership skills. By 1925, the numbers had swelled to over 250,000.

### Organization

The primary organizational structure in the Wayfarer Scouts is the squad. A squad is made up of four to eight scouts who meet regularly to practice skills, earn advancement, participate in service opportunities, or just go adventuring. Squad members may be assigned different responsibilities, but no command structure exists as they are encouraged to cooperate and work together. Each squad chooses a name and a yell for their group. Animal names are common, such as the Red Elk Squad, Sidewinder Squad, or the Gray Wolf Squad (with a squad yell of “Owwww-oooooo”).

A collection of squads linked together by geography, such as a local community, is known as a unit. Adult volunteers oversee each unit, providing guidance, teaching, and advice. The adult in charge is known as the Wayfarer Leader. Other adults who support the leader are called Assistant Leaders. They offer advice and sometimes accompany squads or units on camping trips into the wilderness. Currently, one unit is in **Westhaven** (see **Chapter 3** ). This is Unit #104, which most call the “Westhaven Unit.”

Wayfarer Scouts progress through the program in a structured manner. As scouts learn and demonstrate new skills, they are promoted to higher ranks. Once
a scout turns 18, they age out and most likely move on to higher education, employment, or military service. Some scouts continue or return as adult leaders within the Wayfarer Scouts.

As Captain Corker likes to say, _“A Wayfarer Scout can be easily spotted by their good works.”_ In practice, when Wayfarer Scouts work in the community, they typically wear their uniforms. Wayfarer Scouts wear a heavy, dark-green cotton shirt adorned with numerous badges signifying their rank, skills they have acquired, and adventures they have been on.

Headgear is not required. Scouts that can afford one wear a brimmed felt hat with the Wayfarer Scout logo pin; others wear a simple brown beret.

### Ranger

The Ranger is an experienced Wayfarer Scout and shows a high level of proficiency in all basic skills. They must demonstrate knowledge of the following:

- Cooking (Advanced)
- Edible and poisonous plants
- Fire-building using primitive techniques
- Orienteering (Advanced)
- Wilderness survival

In addition, the Ranger should participate in 15 hours of community service. The Ranger must demonstrate the skills they have accumulated with a solo camping trip; for this, they must plan the gear and food they should bring and select the location. They must hike there and camp alone for one day and night. This typically serves as the last step before Warden.

### Warden

Wayfarer Scouts who successfully navigate the lower ranks earn the rank of Warden. They are typically the oldest, most experienced scouts.

Wardens have demonstrated a strong grasp of the various skills needed by a Wayfarer Scout, camped many nights, and displayed strong leadership skills, including directing at least three community service projects. The Warden teaches younger scouts and passes on their knowledge. Wardens passing all final skill challenges receive the title of Chief Warden, the highest award a Wayfarer Scout can attain.

The Wayfarer Scouts live by a motto of “Be a kind person today.” Their charge is to do a good deed daily. This can be anything from holding the door for someone to shoveling snow for an elderly neighbor. At each meeting, Wayfarer Scouts pledge to uphold the Wayfarer Scout Oath in their daily lives (see **Wayfarer Scout Oath** , page 10).

### The Westhaven Campaign

This book’s four scenarios can be played as one-shots or in sequence to form the Westhaven Campaign. Assuming campaign play, have players create scoutinvestigators of 11–12 years old (Wanderer rank) or pick from the **Pre-Generated Scout-Investigators** (page 174). Play can then begin with the first scenario, **Tremors Below** (page 70).

Each scenario is set two years apart, one for each scouting rank (Wanderer, Rover, Ranger, Warden), and the scout-investigators should age and develop between adventures (see **Scout-Investigator Development Phase** , page 47).

Feel free to incorporate additional scenarios at any stage. **Scenario Seeds** (page 165) are included with this in mind.

As the final scenario opens, Shadow Over Westhaven (page 132), the scout-investigators are 17–18 years old, their scouting days coming to an end.

### Beyond Westhaven

On reaching adulthood, the scout-investigators are ready to become adult investigators (see **Coming of Age: Becoming an Adult Investigator** , page 49), prepared to take on a myriad of _Call of Cthulhu_ scenarios and campaigns.

The adventures of their youth should stand them in good stead, especially as they share the common bond of having been in the same scout squad—a bond that constitutes a unique Investigator Organization (see _Call of Cthulhu: Investigator Handbook_ , page 122).

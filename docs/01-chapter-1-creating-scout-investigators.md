### Chapter 1: Creating Scout-investigators

This chapter provides rules for creating scout-investigators. Alternate investigator creation methods are at the end.

While investigator creation remains largely the same as standard _Call of Cthulhu_ , important customizations exist. Characteristics and skills should reflect a young person rather than a fully formed adult. Backstory is important, especially when creating a group that all know each other.

### The Steps

The five steps to creating a scout-investigator follow:

- **Step One: Generate Characteristics** (nearby).
- **Step Two: Determine Hobby** (page 17).
- **Step Three: Decide Skills & Allocate Skill Points** (page 23).
- **Step Four: Create a Backstory** (page 23).
- **Step Five: Gear & Possessions** (page 28).

### Step One: Generate Characteristics

Use the following rolls to determine each characteristic:

Write the results on the scout-investigator sheet, filling in the half and fifth values alongside the full characteristic percentage:

- Divide the percentage by two, rounding down, for the “half” (Hard) value.
- Divide the percentage by five, rounding down, for the “fifth” (Extreme) value.

**Note:** the half and fifth numbers are used in relation to characteristics and skills in the game. We recommend calculating these up front to ease play. If using the writable PDF version of the scoutinvestigator sheet, it automatically calculates those values for you.

Then roll for Strength (STR), Size (SIZ), and Education (EDU) as dictated by the age of the character, as follows. If playing these scenarios in order, start as an 11- to 12-year-old (Wanderer) scout-investigator.

### _**scout-investigators Aged 11–12 Years (wanderer)**_

- Strength (STR): roll 2D6+ 3, then multiply the result by 2.

- Size (SIZ): roll 2D6+ 3, then multiply the result by 3.

- Constitution (CON): roll 2D6+ 5, then multiply the result by 5.

- Dexterity (DEX): roll 2D6+ 5, then multiply the result by 5.

- Appearance (APP): roll 2D6+ 5, then multiply the result by 5.

- Intelligence (INT): roll 2D6+ 5, then multiply the result by 5.

- Power (POW): roll 2D6+ 5, then multiply the result by 5.

- Education (EDU): roll 2D6+ 3, then multiply the result by 2.

### _**scout-investigators Aged 13–14 Years (rover)**_

- Strength (STR): roll 2D6+ 3, then multiply the result by 3.

- Size (SIZ): roll 2D6+ 5, then multiply the result by 3.

- Education (EDU): roll 2D6+ 3, then multiply the result by 3.

### _**scout-investigators Aged 15–16 Years (ranger)**_

- Strength (STR): roll 2D6+ 4, then multiply the result by 4.

- Size (SIZ): roll 2D6+ 5, then multiply the result by 4.

- Education (EDU): roll 2D6+ 4, then multiply the result by 4.

### _**scout-investigators Aged 17–18 Years (warden)**_

- Strength (STR): roll 2D6+ 4, then multiply the result by 5.

- Size (SIZ): roll 2D6+ 4, then multiply the result by 5.

- Education (EDU): roll 2D6+ 4, then multiply the result by 5.

### Determine Other Attributes

- Luck: roll 2D6+ 6 and multiply by 5.

- **Cool:** equal to POW.
- **Hit points (HP):** add CON and SIZ together, then divide the total by 10 (round down).

***Note:** Sanity points (SAN) are not used in _Campfire Tales_ (see **Cool** , page 43).

### Family Credit Rating

A scout-investigator may earn pocket money, but their general affluence is a product of their family. Family Credit Rating determines two backstory elements that are likely to be relevant in play: their home and possessions. Consider these when imagining the scout’s homelife; check your ideas with the Keeper, then make entries under their backstory for “Home” and “Gear and Possessions.”

Family Credit Rating implies the occupations of the scout-investigator’s parents or legal guardians. It can also create conflict and story. For example, “ **Bob” Harley** (page 57) might not take kindly to penniless kids in his candy shop, whereas he smiles and greets wealthy ones.

- **Magic points (MP):** equal to one-fifth of POW.
- **Damage Bonus (DB)** and **Build:** add STR and SIZ together and look up the total on **Table 1: Damage Bonus & Build** (nearby).
- **Movement Rate (MOV):** compare DEX, STR, and SIZ as per **Table 2: Movement Rates** (nearby).

### _**roll to Determine a Scout-investigator’s Family Credit Rating (d100):**_

- 01: **Penniless**
- 02 – 15: **Poor**
- 16 – 85: **Average**
- 86 – 99: **Wealthy**
- 100: **Rich**

### Penniless

Always hungry and forced to fend for themselves. They are the victim of unfortunate circumstances; clearly, something has gone seriously awry not only for them, but also for society for a child to live in such poverty.

- **Home:** no fixed home, they perhaps lodge with a friend or relative, or are provided for by the state or charity.
- **Possessions:** nothing but the rags they currently wear. Their pockets are empty.

### Poor

Often hungry and rely on handouts. The adults in their lives struggle to provide, perhaps due to illness, disability, or lack of work.

- **Home:** basic. Most likely overcrowded or vacant because everyone is out looking for work.
- **Possessions:** dressed in hand-me-downs. May own a battered old bicycle. They have some string in their pockets.

### Average

They may not have everything they want, but they have everything they need.

- **Home:** a large, comfortable house with a car parked outside. Some domestic help (cleaners, cooks, gardener, etc.). A second place in the country (such as a hunting lodge). Someone works in a higher-end white-collar profession (bank manager, police chief, accountant, etc.)
- **Possessions:** a shiny bicycle, nice clothes, and a wristwatch. They have something they can show off in their pocket, such as that baseball card everyone wants (signed, of course).

### Rich

These kids go to expensive private schools, but may end up mixing with everyone else as a Wayfarer Scout. Try as they might to blend in, their dress, hair, accent, and vocabulary likely give them away.

- **Home:** a large comfortable house set behind a wall and ornate gates. The chauffeur sometimes escorts them. Someone might own a large company or be a member of an influential, affluent family.
- **Possessions:** shiny stuff and lots of it. Their pockets contain a scented handkerchief and two ten-dollar bills.

### Step Two: Determine Hobby

The hobby categories below follow the notion of “what I want to be when I grow up;” many use the adult occupational counterparts as models. A scoutinvestigator may join the Junior Firefighter Brigade because they want to have a career in firefighting when they grow up. The newspaper carrier may wish to become a journalist, while the science club member may aspire to be a scientist.

- **Home:** a comfortable place to live, it may be big or small; tidy or untidy; busy or quiet; whatever it is, it’s home. Someone is employed as a bluecollar professional (skilled laborer, electrician, mechanic, etc.) or lower-end white-collar profession (schoolteacher, police officer, bank clerk, etc.)
- **Possessions:** a bicycle, all necessary school clothes and supplies. In their pockets, they have a pocketknife, some candy, and a folded-up comics’ page.

### Creating Hobbies

### Amateur Sleuth

The amateur sleuth finds crime and detective stories fascinating. They never leave without their trusty magnifying glass and notebook, and have a keen ear for rumors.

- **Obligations:** none.
- **Trusted adult:** librarian, local historical society, local reporter.
- **Skills:** one interpersonal skill (Charm, Fast Talk, Intimidate, or Persuade), Law, Library Use, Listen or Spot Hidden, Locksmith or Psychology, Stealth, Track, any other one skill as personal or era specialty.

Making the cheerleading squad requires fitness and dedication. The competition is fierce. Cheerleaders should know a lot about their team and the sport. As they progress, they may help train newer recruits.

### Farmhand

If raised on a farm or in a rural community, the scoutinvestigator may well find themselves “helping out.” Such work is likely to be low or unpaid and can be physically demanding. Harvest (July to September being the peak times in the US) is an especially busy period when all hands are required.

- **Obligations:** as and when required on the farm.
- **Trusted adult:** local farmers and farm workers, farm supply shops.
- **Skills:** Art/Craft (Farming), one interpersonal skill (Charm, Fast Talk, Intimidate, or Persuade), Mechanical Repair, Natural World, Track, any three other skills as personal or era specialties.
- **Badge:** starts with the **Animal Friendship** , **Fishing** , or **Nature Badge** (pages 30, 31 and 32).

### Junior Actor

This budding thespian loves serious drama, classics, or musicals. They appear in every school drama club production and attend drama camp every summer. Those who can afford it make regular trips to the local theatre for both stage and screen performances. Wealthy families might see shows in Boston or go to the Ziegfield Follies in NYC.

- **Obligations:** regular rehearsals and shows.
- **Trusted adult:** drama teacher, local amateur dramatics groups, actors, and patrons.
- **Skills:** Art/Craft (Acting), Disguise, History, two interpersonal skills (Charm, Fast Talk, Intimidate, or Persuade), Psychology, any two other skills as personal or era specialties.
- **Badge:** starts with the **Public Speaking Badge** (page 32).

- **Trusted adult:** art teacher, local artists.

### Shop Assistant

In the 1920s, people entered the workplace much younger; however, the game expects scout-investigators to remain in school, but perhaps have a part-time job. This may be assisting a family business or working in a local shop or café. Such work usually consists of menial tasks (washing dishes, stocking shelves, etc.).

- **Obligations:** regular shifts (after school or Saturdays mostly).
- **Trusted adult:** proprietor, regular customers.

### Sports Prodigy

The athletic sports prodigy makes everything look easy. They hope someday to be a professional athlete or Olympic competitor. They already overshadow everyone in their chosen school or community sport. When the time comes, they will be awash in college scholarships to come throw, run, or swim.

- **Obligations:** training, practice, and games.
- **Trusted adult:** college recruiters, high school coaches, local business owners, local sports writers.

- **Skills:** Climb, Dodge, Fighting (Brawl), Jump, Swim, Throw, any two other skills as personal or era specialties.

### Street Punk

The street punk may know local criminals (maybe an older sibling or parent/other relative). As they get older, they likely engage with gangs and more significant crime—unless someone helps them change their ways. The street punk might occasionally work as a bagman: delivering illicit goods and returning payment in brown paper bags to their handlers. Some argue that street punks aren’t bad—just misunderstood.

- **Obligations:** running “errands,” following instructions.
- **Trusted adult:** petty criminals, local police (who may seek to redeem them).
- **Skills:** Art/Craft (Acting) or Disguise, one interpersonal skill (Charm, Fast Talk, or Intimidate), Fighting (Brawl), Locksmith or Mechanical Repair, Sleight of Hand, Spot Hidden, Stealth, any other one skill as personal or era specialty.
- **Badge:** starts with the **Cycling Badge** (page 31).

### Young Entrepreneur

The young entrepreneur’s creative, ordered mind brims with ideas for making money, and often has several schemes cooking at once. They may organize the school snack bar or have higher aspirations.

- **Obligations:** meeting customers or potential investors.
- **Trusted adult:** local businesspeople.
- **Skills:** Accounting, Law, Library Use, Listen, Persuade, Spot Hidden, any two other skills as personal or era specialties.
- **Badge:** starts with the **Reading Badge** (page 33).

### Young Scientist

Youngsters interested in the sciences likely belong to the school science club. They participate in fun and exciting science projects and learn about the scientific method. Club members may participate in student competitions to show off their science skills and inventions.

- **Obligations:** club meetings, occasional events.

- **Trusted adult:** local academics and college professors, science teachers.
- **Skills:** any two science specializations, Library Use, one Language (Other), Language (Own), and three other skills as personal or era specialties.

- Badge: starts with the Nature or Radio Badge (pages 32 and 33).

### Upstate League

Most young people must find a career to support themselves and their family—but life is different for these spoiled rich kids, whose lifetime of wealth comes from just by being born into it. For these youth, the only acceptable career club is the networking provided by the Upstate League. Members go to exclusive camps in the summer, spend time doing volunteer work, fundraising, and internships. Almost all are scions of wealthy families.

However, some parents believe it’s important for their offspring to learn about the “commoners”—and one way to do this is joining the Wayfarer Scouts.

To take the Upstate League hobby, a scoutinvestigator’s **Family Credit Rating** (page 16) must be Rich.

- **Obligations:** extra classes, formal dinners, social functions.
- **Trusted adult:** other wealthy families, local political leaders, bankers, lawyers.
- **Skills:** Art/Craft (Any), Appraise, Language (Other), Language (Own), one interpersonal skill (Charm, Fast Talk, Intimidate, Persuade), Ride (Horse), and two other skills as personal or era specialties.
- **Badge:** starts with the **Ability Badge** of your choice (page 30).

### Step Three: Decide Skills & Allocate Skill Points

Scout-investigators begin as Wanderers (aged 11–12) and have 200 points to spend among the eight skills listed for their chosen hobby. Scout-investigators begin with a limited skill set compared to their adult counterparts. While there are no “Personal Interest skills” (see _Call of Cthulhu: Keeper Rulebook,_ page 36), each hobby allows players free choice over at least one skill (listed as a personal or era specialty). Additionally, scouts can earn **Ability Badges** (page 30) in the game, and many badges provide skill boosts.

- **Wanderer:** 200 starting skill points.
- **Rover:** 250 starting skill points.
- **Ranger:** 300 starting skill points.
- **Warden:** 350 starting skill points.

### Step Four: Create a Backstory

Most ideas and abilities that make a scout-investigator interesting and fun to play are a matter of choice—not dice roll results. Think about your scout-investigator and their family background, friends, enemies, and how they live. Perhaps the scout-investigator might be the relative of an adult investigator the player has developed and used in other scenarios!

Six categories populate the backstory section of the scout-investigator sheet—Personal Description, Home, Trusted Adult, Traits, Obligations, and Fears—try to write down at least one entry for each. It’s not essential to have an entry for every category, but the more you define, the more your scout-investigator comes to life. You can add further details or alter existing ones during play.

A scout-investigator’s backstory is a set of short, pithy statements that help guide roleplaying. It helps define your scout-investigator and reminds you how they relate to the world. Also, the Campfire Notes section below the backstory entries can be used to record other information, such as strange encounters, arcane tomes, and artifacts. Spells learned go here.

### Using Random Tables for Inspiration

Sometimes, creating background details on the fly can be difficult. The following lists and random tables provide a way to quickly determine background details—choose from the lists and roll or choose on the tables—or just use them for inspiration.

As appropriate, choose or roll 1D10 on each table. Each option has examples to get you started. Don’t feel constrained to use what you roll; if it doesn’t mesh with your character concept, roll again or choose differently. However, don’t dismiss it out of hand; something that at first seems incongruous may add an unexpected but welcome character twist.

Above all, these random tables serve as a springboard for your imagination. Each dice roll gives you something to embrace or reject; be sure to react and use it to fuel your imagination. Weave the background together to make a credible all-round character.

### Home

Consider your scout-investigator for a moment; write down a few ideas about their home and the people that share it with them. Usually, these are close family members, but it could be distant relatives, an adopted or foster family, or they may be in care. Note the occupation of the scout-investigators parents (or legal guardians).

Refer to **Family Credit Rating** (page 16) when describing the scout’s home and family. The scout’s Family Credit Rating dictates the affluence of the scout’s home. However, finances are not everything. While poverty can make life challenging, wealth does not automatically bring happiness.

A trusted adult treats the scout-investigator with respect and understanding. Select one adult contact per scout-investigator. This can be a contact listed under their chosen hobby, a relative, or other caring adult. Work with your Keeper to come up with a name, occupation, and place in the community. The Keeper could review named non-player characters (NPCs) in the setting or scenario and suggest an appropriate one (see **Trusted Adults in Westhaven** , nearby).

Trusted adults are NPCs controlled by the Keeper. When scout-investigators interact with their trusted adult, the adult should view the scout-investigator in a favorable light. This doesn’t mean that the adult believes everything the scout-investigators say, nor does it guarantee action. For further advice portraying trusted adults, see **Adults in Play** , below. The game effect of this relationship is story-based and does not confer any mechanical advantage.

As with any relationship, it can be soured and even broken. If the Keeper judges the trusted adult feels mistreated, exploited, or disappointed in the scout, they can state that the relationship soured. Record this on your scout-investigator sheet. A scout can get back into their trusted adult’s good books, either through contrition, an outstanding deed, or through the passing of time (see **Scout-Investigator Development Phase** , page 47).

### _**adults in Play**_

A divide exists between the world of children and the world of adults. Emphasizing this divide in _Campfire Tales_ helps differentiate it from regular _Call of Cthulhu,_ and reinforces what it means to be a scout-investigator. The youth and adults in this game are like strangers; their ways differ, and neither side understands the other.

This divide explains why scout-investigators struggle to have their concerns taken seriously by grown-ups. When scout-investigators warn the local deputy about monsters in the old mine, he just ruffles their hair and laughs. When they tell their parents, they are told to stop making up stories and do their chores.

Adults come to believe that their youthful years were different—somehow better—than those of the present day. Adults distrust youth and consider them unreliable and soft. Adults are ready to believe the worst about children, fundamentally believing they lack a sense of right and wrong (how this makes them different to adults is unclear).

### Fears

Each player should list at least one fear in their scoutinvestigator _’_ s backstory. Think of a fear as an image from a nightmare, never forgotten. The selected fear can begin as mild, but becomes more pronounced during play when a player fails a **Cool** roll (page 43). Pick one of the following or roll 1D10. Make the result specific and personal to your investigator:

1. Animals (pick one: dogs, rodents, snakes, etc.)

2. Creepy crawlies (cockroaches, flies, spiders)

3. Dark (guttering candles, unlit cellar, the woods at night)

4. Deprivation (running out of food, water, or other resources)

5. Ghosts (they could be watching right now, graveyards, undead)

6. Heights (bridges, ladders, trees)

7. Medical (blood, hospitals, needles)

8. Social interaction (embarrassment, public speaking, talking to strangers)

10. Water (the deep lake, the rapid stream, the rickety bridge)

### Obligations

Obligations add color to the scout-investigator’s lives and are both a burden and a boon. Adults appear to respect obligations, even though they often ignore their own. A scout-investigator mentioning obligations when talking with an adult can sometimes earn a bonus die on “social” skill rolls (Charm, Fast Talk, Intimidate, or Persuade). Also, prior obligations can be used to avoid new ones— _“Gee, Mr. Marshal, I’d like to help clean your drains, but I have rehearsal today.”_ Scout-investigators often team up—safety lies in numbers, after all. Sometimes, however, the Keeper may call upon an obligation to divide the scoutinvestigators— _“Say, didn’t you tell Mr. Marshal you have to stay late for rehearsal? Later that evening, it’s getting dark when you cycle home. That’s when you hear the howl, and something running along the road behind you!”_ Most **Hobbies** (page 17) have suggested obligations. Alternatively, they may have an obligation to someone else, such as doing chores, running errands, helping with the family business, and so on. Check your ideas with the Keeper, then add them under backstory for Obligations.

### Scout Badges

Part of being a scout is learning a variety of skills and knowledge. Scout badges mark their progress and achievement in their chosen areas. New badges are gained in the **Scout-Investigator Development Phase** (page 47). The two types are rank and ability badges:

- Rank badges: start at Wanderer (11–12 years old), and progress with age up to the rank of Warden (17–18 years old). Rank provides one or more special benefits once per scenario.

- **Ability badges:** provide a one-off skill increase and a special benefit once per scenario.

On the sheet, both rank and ability badges have two check boxes; one box notes the badge has been “Earned,” and one checks when its special benefit has been used in game. During a **Scout-Investigator Development Phase** (page 47), erase all “Used” checkmarks, indicating those special benefits are once more available.

### Starting Badges

All starting players begin play with the Wayfarer Scout Badge (page 29), the **Wanderer Badge** (page 29), and one **Ability Badge** (page 30) as specified by their hobby. If creating an older, experienced scout-investigator, choose a higher badge— **Rover**, **Ranger**, or **Warden** (nearby) and two or more (at the Keeper’s discretion) **Ability Badges** (page 30).

### Rank Badges

The five rank badges are earned as a scout-investigator gets older. When a rank badge is gained, the player should check the “Earned” box on their scoutinvestigator sheet.

### Wayfarer Scout Badge

All player possess this badge. Being a Wayfarer Scout is about fellowship and helping others. To reflect this, players can spend Luck points to:

- Improve a skill roll made by another squad member (standard _Call of Cthulhu_ players can only spend Luck points on their own skill rolls).
- A player can spend unlimited Luck points for this purpose; this ability can be used multiple times during a scenario.


### Wanderer Badge

11- and 12-year-old scout-investigators begin with this badge. Quite useful, it automatically gains a successful outcome on any skill roll or opposed skill roll, _once per scenario_ . This can only be used on their own roll and cannot be used on behalf of another player. When the Keeper calls for a roll, regardless of difficulty, players may check their Wanderer badge to convert failure into success.

Note, this does not allow players to achieve the impossible: it only works when the Keeper allows a skill roll.

> **Example:** the Keeper states that leaping the chasm requires a Jump roll and an Extreme success—the player fails the roll but decides to check their Wanderer badge to successfully land on the far side. A scout couldn't leap the Grand Canyon regardless of their dice rolls, so checking their badge would not help.

### Rover Badge

13- and 14-year-old scout-investigators begin with this badge. The Rover badge replaces the Wanderer badge, and the game benefits of the Wanderer badge are no longer available to the player.

Use the Rover badge to refresh a player’s own Luck points. Once per scenario, the player may check their Rover badge, roll 2D6+6, and multiply the result by 5. They may use the new value to replace their scoutinvestigator _’_ s current Luck score.


### Ranger Badge
15- and 16-year-old scout-investigators begin with this badge. The Ranger badge replaces the Rover badge, and the game benefits of the Rover badge are no longer available to the player.

Use the Ranger badge to gain one bonus die on one of their own skill rolls, once per scenario. Before a skill roll is made, the player may take one bonus die.

- If the roll is successful, the player must check their scout-investigator _’_ s Ranger badge.
- If the roll is unsuccessful, the badge is not checked, and the player may take a bonus die on a future roll.


### Warden Badge

17- and 18-year-old scout-investigators begin with this badge. The Warden badge replaces the Ranger badge, and the game benefits of the Ranger badge are no longer available to the player.

Wardens spend much time helping others. Use the Warden badge to give one bonus die to someone else, once per scenario. Before a skill roll is made, the player may give another player one bonus die.

- If the roll is successful, the player must check their Warden badge.
- If the roll is unsuccessful, the badge is not checked, and their player may give one bonus die on a future roll.

### The Scout Squad: an Investigator Organization

Investigator Organizations (see _Call of Cthulhu: Investigator Handbook_ , page 122) provide players with a framework on which to construct meaningful, long-term groups. In _Campfire Tales_ , the investigator organization is the scout squad, and the scoutinvestigators all belong. Players should decide on a name for their squad (e.g. Red Robins, Mad Wolves, Tin Lizzies, etc.), as well as their squad’s call—have fun with the players and encourage them to routinely use their squad’s call!

A squad consists of four to eight scouts. The group may wish to include NPC scouts in their squad, especially if you have fewer than four players. Use the **Ready-to-Play Scout-investigators** or create some from scratch. NPC squad members are usually played by the Keeper. However, with everyone’s agreement, a player may play one as a replacement or stand-in for their own investigator.

**Note: Assisted Fighting Maneuvers** (page 38) are most effective when performed by four individuals, hence the above suggestion.

Make it somewhere memorable, somewhere with character.

- At a squad member’s home, perhaps their basement, attic, garden shed, or treehouse.
- At an abandoned house or cabin; again, perhaps a cellar, attic, or maybe the whole property.
- At a den the squad built, such as a treehouse in the woods.

If the base is in someone’s home, household members may interfere in some way or overhear the squad’s discussions. If the base is outside the security of the family home, it becomes vulnerable in other ways; depending on location, other children or adults may discover it, and they could trash the place or invade.

### Resources

Over time, the squad collects resources. These can be mundane items, such as candles, maps, and snacks. Or they may be rarities. For example, **Boxcar Jim** (page 62) could present scout-investigators with trinkets and charms. These may be collected by the squad and stored either at their base or their homes.

### Optional Rules

Encourage discussion about the squad and decide issues, such as a **Squad Leader** (nearby), a **Base** (below), and **Resources** (nearby). These largely provide background color, but may become relevant in play.

### Squad Leader

The Wayfarer Scouts organization relies upon volunteers from the community for assistant scout leaders. They attend scout meetings, help with activities, hikes, and camps. One responsible adult is assigned to each squad and is known as their squad leader. A scout can go to their squad leader when in need; however, remember that the squad leaders are just regular people who volunteered and have their own family and work obligations.

### Base

Every squad has a base, a place to hang out. This can be negotiated between the Keeper and the players.

### Other Ways of Creating Scout-investigators

Different methods of scout-investigator creation exist, and different groups may agree on different methods. Some prefer a random approach; others prefer varying degrees of freedom to modify values, perhaps making characteristics fit a preconceived character concept or their perception of game balance.

For these reasons, alternative scout-investigator creation methods for creating are detailed here. Discuss with your group and decide which method or combination of methods best suits them.

### _**option 1: Placing Rolled Characteristics**_

Roll and record five rolls. Allocate the values as you wish among the five characteristics—CON, DEX, APP, INT, and POW. Do the same for the other three rolls for STR, SIZ, and EDU—as appropriate to the scout’s age (see **Step One: Generate Characteristics** , page 15). Allot the values as you wish among those three characteristics.

### _**option 2: Point-buy Characteristics**_

If you prefer to spend points rather than rolling dice, a total of 300 points can be spent among the five characteristics (CON, DEX, APP, INT, POW), each in the range of 35–85. The three remaining characteristics (STR, SIZ, EDU) depend on the age of the scout being created:

- Wanderer (11-12 years of age): spend 70 points among STR, SIZ, and EDU, each in the range of 10–45.

- Rover (13-14 years of age): spend 95 points among STR, SIZ, and EDU, each in the range of 15–50.

- Ranger (15-16 years of age): spend 135 points among STR, SIZ, and EDU, each in the range of 25–70.

- Warden (17-18 years of age): spend 165 points among STR, SIZ, and EDU, each in the range of 30–80.

- Rover: allocate 40, 50, 60, 70, 80 where you like among the five characteristics: CON, DEX, APP, INT, POW. For the remaining three characteristics (STR, SIZ, EDU), allocate 25, 30, 40 among them.

- Ranger: allocate 40, 50, 60, 70, 80 where you like among the five characteristics: CON, DEX, APP, INT, POW. For the remaining three characteristics (STR, SIZ, EDU), allocate 35, 45, 55 among them.

- Warden: allocate 40, 50, 50, 50, 60, 60, 70, 80 as you wish among the eight characteristics.

### Family Credit Rating: Alternative Method

For those who prefer to choose their scoutinvestigator’s **Family Credit Rating** (page 16), use this method. Choose the family credit rating and adjust the scout-investigators skill points accordingly:

- **Penniless:** gain an additional 20 skill points.

### _**option 3: Quick-fire Method**_

Use this method to get up and running quickly.

- **Poor:** gain an additional 10 skill points.
- **Average:** no change in skill points.
- **Wealthy:** reduce skill points by 20.

- Wanderer: allocate 40, 50, 60, 70, 80 where you like among the five characteristics: CON, DEX, APP, INT, POW. For the remaining three characteristics (STR, SIZ, EDU), allocate 20, 25, 25 among them.

- **Rich:** reduce skill points by 50.

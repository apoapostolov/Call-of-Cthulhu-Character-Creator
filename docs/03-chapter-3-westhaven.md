### Chapter 3: Westhaven

hese scenarios take place in the fictional town of Westhaven, located 40 miles (64 T km) west of Arkham, Massachusetts. This idyllic town sits near the New Hampshire border and abuts great forests that stretch north for miles. As you head north out of Westhaven, the increasingly larger hills turn into small mountains, the White Mountains of New Hampshire.

The Atlantic Ocean seacoasts can be reached quickly by turning east and traveling through Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Rhode Island, or Maine. Many working fishing villages dot the coastline, bringing in whitefish from the cold Atlantic or delicious lobsters and clams from the coast.

### History of Westhaven

Westhaven was founded in 1689 by a breakaway Puritan sect who disagreed on the source of divine providence. Rumors swirled among the locals that the sect’s true motivations were more sinister. Some accused the Westhaven group of collaborating with dark forces. As a result, Westhaven became isolated and shunned by neighboring towns for decades. Despite such isolation, or perhaps because of it, many tragic events occurred during the following years.

The Salem Witch Trials reached their zenith in the late 17[th] century, gripping the region in terror; many residents feared for their lives. Unfortunately, Westhaven also gave in to this madness. Samuel Minter, a theologian, arrived in 1693 and convinced local elders he was drawn by, “ _a sickening smell of witchcraft permeating the area._ ”

Minter studied with Cotton Mather, and he put on a showman-like display that captivated locals. His fiery public sermons and tales of hunting the “ _devil’s brides_ ” enthralled and terrified townsfolk. During the first scenario, **Tremors Below** (page 70), scoutinvestigators may find a tome penned by Minter.

### Westhaven in 1918

Westhaven’s population is 3, 270, making it larger than many neighbors, some of which only have a few hundred residents. The town’s elected officials pass local ordinances and maintain the town operations and finances. The town council is presided over by a president elected by the council from within their ranks.

The current town president, Nathan Booker (page 58), owns a local business and is a family man. While genial, he is not a particularly strong leader and often defers to council vice-president, **Thomas Cline** (page 60). Cline maintains a vast network of contacts and uses those connections to his advantage. He also secretly belongs to the **Sons of Seth** (page 60) and features prominently in the final scenario in this book, **Shadow Over Westhaven** (page 132).

Westhaven is vibrant by most standards. The Spring Festival celebrates the renewal of the Earth; however, its odd way of celebrating the “death of winter” carries strange, somber tones. No one knows why, it has always been that way.

The Independence Day celebration is especially festive, complete with loud fireworks to end the day. Children decorate their bikes with streamers and parade down Main Street to the delight of parents and local shop owners. Every fall, the harvest parade wends through town, complete with horse- and ox-drawn floats.

### 2. Middlewich College

One reason for the town’s relative prosperity is Middlewich College. This small, liberal arts private school was founded in 1822 by Herbert H. Bottoms. Bottoms was a local business leader who moved from Ireland as a young man and prospered in timber. He was keen on education due to his mother being a schoolteacher back in Derry.

Bottoms also donated money for the town library, which bears his name. While many people attend Middlewich College seeking teaching or business degrees, it has also become well-known for its Antiquities and Archeology Department.

### 3. Harley’s Sweet Shoppe

The ice cream parlor (or soda fountain) is one of the most iconic meeting spots of the early 20[th] century. Harley’s is a quintessential example of this type of establishment and a social anchor of the community. The shop’s owner, Bob Harley, is outgoing and friendly. Bob makes ice cream and a wide variety of confections from scratch; however, his cream sodas are by far his customers’ favorite. The vanilla-pistachio ice cream soda is his best-seller.

The interior features a 30 foot (9 m) long service counter with round stools fixed to the floor. A soda fountain and ice cream coolers sit behind the counter. Black- and white-checkered tile adorns the floor. Bright polished chrome and decorative light sconces festoon the walls.

**Notable NPC: Robert “Bob” Harley** (opposite).

### 4. Westhaven Central Park

In the center of Westhaven, just off Main Street, lies a five-acre park created in the town’s early days as a place to share garden plots. Later, it transitioned into a recreational space with open fields and wooded areas. This popular destination for families sees lots of traffic on weekends and holidays. The park also houses the town baseball field that the junior league uses for its games.

### 5. Palladium Theater

At night or on Saturday afternoons (especially when it’s raining), locals flock here to see the latest releases from Hollywood. Hitting the theater recently are silent classics like as _Cleopatra_ (starring Theda Bara), _Easy Street_ (starring Charlie Chaplin), and _The Butcher Boy_ (featuring Fatty Arbuckle and Buster Keaton).

### 6. Bailey’s Top-quality Hardware

One of the many Top-Quality Hardware franchises, Bailey’s has been a Westhaven staple for decades. Started in 1887 by Duncan Bailey, the firm was passed down to his son, Ted; now, his grandson, Bertram runs the place. Everyone calls him “Bert.” Like many hardware stores of the day, it functions as a department store for small towns; this allows rural residents to purchase goods usually only available in urban areas.

Scout-investigators likely visit Bailey’s regularly. They sell fishing tackle and bait, toys and candy, the latest adventure magazine, and baseball mitts. The scouts know Bert and many old-timers that tarry to tell hunting, fishing, and war stories. This makes Bailey’s a tremendous resource of local information that scoutinvestigators can tap when needed.

### 7. Starlite Diner

The Starlite is known for its homemade pies and country cooking. A breakfast of eggs, fried potatoes, bacon, and toast usually starts the day. Their lunch special sandwich is fried liver and onions with slaw. The dinner menu boasts turkey on toast with gravy, which is also their best seller. Country apple with hard cheese is their most popular pie, but the rhubarb, when in season, is also a crowd pleaser.

A marble lunch counter with stools for customers runs nearly the length of the interior. Foot-square light green tiles cover the floor, and oversized brass light fixtures grace the walls. The front windows have large decorative booths where patrons can sit while they eat and chat.

Behind the main lunch counter, Sandy Turner slings coffee. Behind her, steam from the grill can be seen through a large window into the kitchen area. Back in the kitchen, Leonard Abraham does most cooking. On busy days, several wait staff work the room, including Sheila and Maude Turner.

### **boxcar Jim,** _**age 44**_ **,** _**transient & Friend**_


Boxcar Jim is a strange fellow. He appears to be a destitute “hobo” (vagrant). However, his shabby exterior hides a man who seems to have lived several lifetimes and seen truly bizarre sights on his adventures.

Boxcar Jim was born James Cavendish in Tombstone, Arizona. He grew up on a hog farm, getting up early, doing chores, and then going to school. Jim dreamed of seeing the far-off places he read about at school.

Jim left at 16, deciding to travel the world and see those wondrous far-off, exotic places. He started by hitching rides on rail cars and traveling across the US, ultimately making his way to New York City. From there, Jim scraped up enough money for steerage passage to Europe.

In 1901, Jim took a steam ship from southern France to western Africa, docking in Dakar in what is now Senegal. From there, he wandered down the western coast, making his way to present day South Africa. Jim had exhausted all his money when he decided to sign up as a mercenary to fight in the Second Boer War. This decision would impact him deeply.

Boxcar Jim‘s nickname comes from his method of traveling around the region in the boxcars of freight trains. He typically stays in a town until he can no longer get supplies or the townsfolk tire of him. This usually lasts a couple of weeks, sometimes longer. He might be gone for months, but eventually returns to Westhaven and repeats the cycle.

### Banish the Visitor

- Cost: 20 magic points; 20 Luck points* (both costs to be shared by the casters)

- **Casting Time:** 1 hour

This spell can trap a trans-dimensional human or human-like intelligence who traveled here from another dimension. It also works on creatures who should no longer be here, such as ghosts. It fails against creatures commanded by another intelligence.

At least three people must participate in the casting. All participants must know the spell. More participants increase the effectiveness. This spell is powered by destroying, with ritual flames, a physical representation of the target (like a voodoo doll); and it requires a glass bottle with a cork stopper.

The spell must be cast in a place of power associated with the target; this could be known haunts, its last known location, or its point of entry into this dimension. For the spell to take effect, win an opposed POW roll between the caster with the highest POW and the target. Certain bonuses can be gained to the casting (maximum of two):

- Grant one bonus die for casting in the middle of the night (12–3 am).
- Grant one bonus die if the caster possesses something that belonged to the target (e.g., hair, fingernails, bones, personal journal, or their jewelry).
- Grant one bonus die if doubling the magic and Luck point expenditure (a total combined cost to the casters of 40 magic points and 40 Luck points).

Casters must divide their numbers: at least one must remain outside a protective circle while the rest stand within. The spell’s target cannot enter the protective circle during casting. If the spell succeeds, the target gets trapped in the bottle, provided the bottle remains unbroken and the cork stopper in place.

Jim acquired this spell during the Second Boer War. It is written on a rolled-up parchment and stuffed into a small glass bottle with a cork stopper.

### Immolate

• Cost: 5 magic points; 10 **Luck** points*

- **Casting time:** 1 round

Reciting this spell and flinging a pinch of ash into the air, produces a torch-size ball of flame. This ball erupts on the targeted creature or an object within 30 feet (9 m) of the caster. Anything flammable immediately ignites and burns until extinguished, causing 1D6 damage at the end of each round. It takes a full action to douse the flames. If nothing is flammable (e.g., a stone statue) the flame dies.

Jim found this spell while rummaging through the trash cans outside **Mortimer’s** (page 59) one evening. As he dined on the remains of a pork chop, he observed two well-dressed gentlemen smoking outside as they left the restaurant. One handed the other a cocktail napkin, which then went in the pocket of the other man’s suit jacket.

As they parted ways, the man reached into his jacket for matches to light his cigarette, causing the folded napkin to fall to the ground. Unaware of this, the man walked briskly into the night. The napkin blew over to Jim, who picked it up and read it. Realizing the words’ importance, he stuffed it into his pocket for later.

Initially, this spell comes on a crumpled-up cocktail napkin, but scout-investigators may transcribe it onto something sturdier.
